# Dog bites: reporting and civil claims

A dog bite is distressing for the victim and complicated for everyone involved. This page explains what to do and how the agencies divide responsibility.

## If you have just been bitten

1. Get away from the dog. If possible, get to a safe place indoors.
2. Wash the wound with soap and running water for 5 minutes.
3. Apply pressure to stop bleeding.
4. Get medical advice. Call 111 for puncture wounds, deep cuts, bites that need stitching, or any bite to a child, a person who is immunosuppressed, or an older person. Call 999 for severe bleeding or facial injury.
5. If practical, take photos of the wound, the dog, and the location.
6. Get the dog owner's details if it is safe to do so: name, address, phone, microchip if visible.

You may need a tetanus booster (every 10 years for adults) and antibiotics. Rabies is extremely unlikely from a UK dog but the GP/A&E will assess risk.

## Reporting the bite

The decision tree is:

| Situation | Report to |
|-----------|-----------|
| You were attacked in a public place or your own home, and the dog acted aggressively | 999 (immediate threat) or 101 (after the fact) |
| You were bitten by your own dog at home | GP for medical advice; not a police matter |
| You were bitten while working (delivery driver, postal worker, healthcare visit) | Your employer; HSE under RIDDOR if injury required time off |
| The dog has run loose and is intimidating others | 101 or 01632 960140 |

Police take the lead on prosecution under the Dangerous Dogs Act 1991. The Council supports with Community Protection Notices and welfare enquiries.

## Civil claims

If you wish to claim compensation, you have two main routes:

### Owner's third-party insurance

Most home contents insurance policies include a third-party liability section that may cover dog bites. Ask the dog's owner for their insurance details. They are not legally obliged to give them, but most do.

If the dog is an XL Bully under exemption (since February 2024), specific third-party insurance is mandatory.

### Personal injury solicitor

Civil claims for dog bite injuries are usually brought under the Animals Act 1971. Liability is complicated and depends on whether the keeper knew of the dog's dangerous characteristics. Time limits: 3 years from the date of injury (or from age 18 for child victims).

The Council does not provide solicitor recommendations. The Law Society's "Find a Solicitor" service at www.lawsociety.org.uk includes filters for personal injury and animal-related claims.

## Investigation and evidence

If you want a council role in the case (for example, to support a Community Protection Notice), tell us:

- Date, time, and location of the incident
- Description of the dog (size, breed, colour, distinguishing marks)
- Owner's name and address if known
- Witness contact details
- Photos and medical evidence
- Whether the police are already involved (incident reference)

We can request information from the police about previous incidents involving the same dog or owner. Where there is a pattern, a CPN or PSPO breach may be appropriate.

## Children

Children are disproportionately affected by dog bites. Most bites to children are from a known dog (family pet, friend's dog, neighbour's dog) and happen to the face. Health Visitors share dog safety advice during 1-year and 2-year reviews; the Council's Family Hubs run free "Dogs and Children" sessions twice a year.

## Workers

Postal workers, delivery drivers, district nurses, social workers, and meter readers are at high risk. Aldershire works with employers (Royal Mail, Aldershire Cares, Severn Valley Water) on a voluntary "Dog Aware Address" register. Properties on the register get pre-visit warnings to their staff.

## Statutory background

Dangerous Dogs Act 1991 sections 3 and 4. Animals Act 1971 sections 2-6. Animal Welfare Act 2006. Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 (RIDDOR) for work-related bites.
