# Self-neglect cases

Self-neglect is one of the categories of abuse and neglect under the Care Act 2014. It covers a wide range of behaviour from neglecting personal hygiene, health, or surroundings to behaviour such as hoarding (covered separately).

Self-neglect is unusual within safeguarding because the person is not being harmed by another. The conduct is the person's own. This makes it ethically and operationally challenging: respecting the adult's right to make unwise decisions about their own life sits in tension with our duty to protect adults at risk.

## When self-neglect is a safeguarding concern

The Care and Support Statutory Guidance (paragraphs 14.16-14.17) confirms that self-neglect can fall within section 42 if:

- The adult has needs for care and support, **and**
- The adult is experiencing, or is at risk of, harm as a result, **and**
- Their care and support needs mean they are unable to protect themselves.

Many older people in Aldershire have homes that are cluttered, dirty, or in disrepair. Self-neglect crosses into safeguarding when there is a risk of serious harm: untreated wounds or pressure sores, malnutrition, fire risk from extreme clutter, refusal of essential medication, repeated falls.

## Multi-agency response

Self-neglect cases are rarely solved by a single intervention. Aldershire convenes a **Self-Neglect Multi-Agency Risk Management (MARM) meeting** where threshold is met. Standing attendees:

- Adult social worker (chair).
- GP or community matron.
- District nurse, mental health worker (where engaged).
- Housing provider.
- Fire & Rescue (Aldershire Fire and Rescue Service Home Safety team).
- Environmental health (for property condition risks).
- Family or trusted neighbour, with the adult's consent.

The MARM produces a single coordinated plan with a named lead worker.

## Engagement-based approach

Aldershire follows the engagement-based approach set out in research by Braye, Orr, and Preston-Shoot (2014, updated 2020):

- Build a trusting relationship over time. Multiple short visits at the adult's pace are more effective than a single long intervention.
- Find what the adult themselves wants to change. Connect our offer to their stated goals.
- Use small, reversible steps. A single skip on the drive may achieve more than a deep clean.
- Keep working at it. Self-neglect cases that progress over years are normal, not a failure.

## When statutory powers are used

In most cases the council does not impose intervention. The adult has the right to refuse. Statutory powers come into play only where:

- The adult lacks **capacity** to make decisions about their care (Mental Capacity Act 2005 best interests pathway).
- The dwelling is in such condition that **environmental health enforcement** under Part 3 of the Environmental Protection Act 1990 or the Housing Act 2004 is justified (filthy and verminous premises, statutory nuisance).
- There is **fire risk** justifying Fire & Rescue intervention.
- A **wider safeguarding concern** (financial abuse, modern slavery, undue influence by another person) emerges.

The council does not pursue eviction or compulsory removal as a self-neglect response. Where institutional admission is in best interests (e.g. an adult lacking capacity who is at imminent risk), the DOLS or Court of Protection routes apply.

## Bereavement and life-event triggers

Self-neglect often follows bereavement, the loss of a partner who was the household's organiser, retirement, or a fall. We aim to identify these transitions early. The council's preventive **Aldershire Wellbeing Visit** offers a free home visit to anyone aged 75+ or recently bereaved; book on **01632 960 460**.
